# Timing and persuasive copy

## Every second has a job; not every second needs a cut

Use a continuous time axis from 0 to the chosen runtime. Mark opening events precisely, including the first visible frame. Every interval adds information, relevance, evidence, comprehension time, or action. A held result has a job; ornamental transitions that delay value often do not.

Ask what the viewer now knows, and why the next beat is worth watching. Separate persuasion from execution: "resolve setup anxiety" is the job; "show real first use in two taps" is the shot. Change pattern with purpose: new proof, close-up, reaction, revealing detail, sound, perspective. Random zooms can compete with the demo. Pacing includes sentence rhythm and discovery, not just cut frequency.

## Speech and reading budgets

The deck suggests roughly 80 words in 30 seconds (160 words/minute). Treat it as a draft estimate. A rough English/Indonesian planning range of 120–160 words/minute leaves different room for pauses, difficult terms, and interaction. This range is an editorial heuristic, not a research result. Time a native read-through; word counts do not transfer reliably across languages.

Budget less speech when viewers must read UI or absorb a number. Match emphasis to visible events. If a line does not fit, cut it, extend the shot, or simplify the argument; do not default to faster delivery.

Separate subtitles from emphasis overlays. Avoid unrelated narration, detailed UI, and a long headline simultaneously. Keep one focal point. Preview at phone size, allowing text to appear, be read, and resolve; animation time is not reading time.

## Copy that carries the argument

- Use buyer language and concrete situations, not "revolutionize your experience."
- Connect features to consequences through a visible mechanism. Show the learner answering a prompt rather than listing "speaking practice."
- Derive specificity from evidence. Never invent percentages, milestones, speed claims, comparison prices, or time-to-result.
- A demo proves the shown task under shown conditions; it does not prove universal accuracy, fluency, revenue, or long-term outcomes.
- Write speech as short, natural clauses from an identifiable point of view; avoid press-release dialogue.
- Let visuals carry obvious actions; narration can explain why they matter.
- Keep one main promise with supporting reasons. Unrelated features often deserve separate ads.
- Stage a demonstration honestly; do not present acting as a customer's real experience.

## Claims

Use a claim ledger: exact claim → source/evidence → allowed wording → asset → status. When unsupported, offer a demonstrable alternative: "try a practice conversation" instead of "speak fluently in seven days."

Price, trial length, cancellation, eligibility, renewal, availability, and comparisons must match the actual offer/destination. Scarcity requires a real basis; occasion/task relevance can motivate action without a deadline. Label synthetic FAQ cards as questions, not user comments. Do not exploit shame or sensitive traits merely to gain attention.

## CTA

Write a concrete action and immediate benefit where helpful: "Open the demo and try one conversation." Match spoken ask, overlay, platform button, and destination. "Try free" needs a real free path and material terms. Explain whether viewers open a demo, pricing page, app store, or form.

Allow reading and action time. Introduce the action before the end when useful; repeat it instead of adding competing asks. URL, QR code, sticker, and "link below" are placement-dependent; specify the actual route.
